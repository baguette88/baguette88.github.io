How to Spot Fake Retro Game Cartridges: A Complete Authentication Guide

By Baguette Tools · February 26, 2026 · 10 min read

Counterfeit retro game cartridges have become one of the biggest problems in the collecting hobby. As prices for authentic games have risen, the market for reproductions and bootlegs has grown alongside it. Some fakes are sold openly as reproductions at low prices, which is fine if that is what you want. The real danger is counterfeits sold as authentic games at authentic prices. Learning to tell the difference is an essential skill for any retro game collector, and the techniques are straightforward once you know what to look for.

Visual Inspection: The First Line of Defense

Label Quality

The label is the first thing to examine and often the quickest way to identify a fake. Genuine cartridge labels have specific characteristics that reproductions struggle to match exactly:

  • Print quality: Authentic labels use professional offset printing with smooth gradients and sharp text. Reproduction labels are typically inkjet or laser printed, resulting in visible dot patterns when examined under magnification. Look at fine text like the copyright line at the bottom of the label. Genuine labels print this text crisply. Fakes often produce blurry or slightly pixelated fine text.
  • Color saturation: Genuine labels have consistent, vivid colors. Reproductions frequently have colors that are slightly off, either oversaturated or washed out compared to the original. If you have access to a known-authentic copy, a side-by-side comparison makes color differences obvious.
  • Finish and texture: Most genuine Nintendo labels have a specific semi-gloss finish. Some games (particularly later Game Boy Advance titles) have a holographic element or a specific textured finish. Reproductions often use a different finish that is either too glossy or too matte.
  • Official seal: The "Official Nintendo Seal of Quality" (or "Official Nintendo Seal" on later releases) is a reliable checkpoint. Genuine seals are printed with precise metallic gold ink. On fakes, the seal is often the wrong shade of gold, slightly blurry, or missing fine detail in the border pattern.

Shell Quality and Mold Marks

The cartridge shell itself carries authentication clues that counterfeiters rarely replicate accurately:

  • Plastic color and texture: Genuine Nintendo cartridges use specific plastic formulations. An authentic Game Boy cartridge has a particular shade of grey with a smooth, slightly textured finish. Reproductions often use a slightly different shade, and the plastic may feel smoother, rougher, or have a different density when held.
  • Mold markings: Turn the cartridge over. Genuine cartridges have a number stamped or molded into the back of the shell, typically in the lower right or center. This is the mold number used during manufacturing. The quality of this stamp on genuine carts is consistent and clean. Reproductions either omit this marking, use a different numbering system, or produce a blurry stamp.
  • Screw type: Nintendo used proprietary gamebit security screws on most cartridges. Game Boy, SNES, and N64 cartridges all use 3.8mm gamebit screws. Many reproductions use standard Phillips screws or tri-wing screws instead. A Phillips screw on a Game Boy cartridge is an immediate red flag.
  • Shell fit and seam quality: Genuine shells have tight seams with minimal gaps. Reproduction molds are less precise, often resulting in visible gaps along the shell seam, uneven edges, or a shell that does not sit flush when assembled.
Getting more sophisticated: High-quality reproductions from 2024 onward have improved significantly in shell and label quality. The external visual checks described above will catch the majority of fakes, but the most convincing counterfeits require opening the cartridge to inspect the circuit board for definitive authentication.

Circuit Board Analysis: The Definitive Test

Opening the cartridge and examining the circuit board is the most reliable way to determine authenticity. You will need a 3.8mm gamebit screwdriver (under $8 from any electronics or game tool retailer) and good lighting.

What Genuine Boards Look Like

  • PCB color: Genuine Nintendo boards are green. This applies to Game Boy, Game Boy Color, Game Boy Advance, SNES, and N64 cartridges. Reproduction boards are commonly blue, purple, red, or black. A non-green board is almost always a reproduction.
  • Nintendo markings: Genuine boards have "Nintendo" printed on the PCB along with a board revision number (such as "DMG-BEAN-10" for a Game Boy board). The text is clean, professionally printed, and consistent across the board. Reproductions either omit these markings or print them with different fonts and positioning.
  • Chip types: Genuine cartridges use standard IC packages (small rectangular chips with visible pins). Reproductions frequently use glob-top chips, which appear as black epoxy blobs covering the chip die directly on the board. A glob-top chip on any Nintendo cartridge from the Game Boy through N64 era is a definitive sign of a reproduction.
  • Component count and layout: Genuine boards have a specific number of chips arranged in a known layout for each game. A ROM chip, a memory controller, and sometimes a battery for save data. Reproductions often have different chip counts, different layouts, or additional components like a microSD card slot that obviously does not belong on a 1990s game cartridge.

Battery and Save Functionality

Many retro games use a CR2025 or CR2032 battery to maintain save data. On genuine boards, the battery is soldered to the PCB with tabbed battery clips. The battery brand and cell date can provide additional authentication clues. Reproduction boards sometimes use battery holders instead of soldered tabs, or they may not include a battery at all, relying on flash memory for saves instead.

Photo documentation tip: If you are buying online and cannot open the cartridge before purchase, ask the seller for a photo of the circuit board. Reputable sellers who deal in authentic games will typically accommodate this request. A seller who refuses or claims they cannot open the cartridge is a red flag. Testing with Hardware

Beyond visual inspection, there are functional tests that can reveal fakes:

  • Boot behavior: Some reproductions boot with a noticeably longer delay than genuine cartridges, or they may display a brief flash of garbled graphics before the game loads. This is because the reproduction's flash memory chip has different access timing than the original ROM chip.
  • Save reliability: Play the game, save your progress, turn off the console, and turn it back on. Genuine cartridges with a good battery will retain the save reliably. Some reproductions have inconsistent save behavior, losing data between power cycles or corrupting saves after extended play.
  • Game Boy Player and Super Game Boy: Some Game Boy and GBC reproductions do not function correctly in the Super Game Boy (SNES) or Game Boy Player (GameCube) accessories. If the game works in a handheld but fails or glitches in these accessories, it may be a reproduction with incomplete hardware compatibility.
  • GBA flash cart detection: The Nintendo DS can detect certain GBA reproduction cartridges and will refuse to boot them, displaying an error message. This is not a universal test, but it catches some lower-quality fakes.

The Most Commonly Counterfeited Games

Counterfeiters target the most valuable and recognizable titles because these offer the highest profit margin. Be especially vigilant when purchasing any of these games:

Pokemon Games (All Platforms)

Pokemon cartridges are by far the most commonly counterfeited retro games. Pokemon Emerald, FireRed, LeafGreen, Ruby, and Sapphire for the GBA are the worst offenders, with estimates suggesting that a significant percentage of these titles sold on secondary markets are reproductions. Pokemon Crystal, Gold, and Silver for the GBC are also heavily counterfeited. Always open the cartridge and verify the board before paying authentic prices for any Pokemon game.

Zelda Games

The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past (SNES), Ocarina of Time (N64), Majora's Mask (N64), and The Minish Cap (GBA) are all commonly faked. Zelda reproductions tend to be higher quality than average, making board inspection especially important.

Mario Games

Super Mario World (SNES), Super Mario 64 (N64), and Super Mario Advance titles (GBA) all have significant reproduction presence in the market. Mario games are counterfeited less aggressively than Pokemon because the authentic copies are more affordable, but fakes still circulate widely.

High-Value Rarities

Any game worth over $100 loose should be treated with extra scrutiny. Titles like EarthBound (SNES), Hagane (SNES), Little Samson (NES), Shantae (GBC), and Mega Man X3 (SNES) are all targets for sophisticated counterfeits priced to exploit collectors who are excited about finding a rare game at a good price.

Where to Buy Authentic Cartridges

Reducing your exposure to counterfeits starts with choosing the right sources:

  • Established retro game retailers: Sellers who specialize in retro games, like JB Toyz, build their reputation on selling authentic products. They have the expertise to identify fakes before listing them and the business incentive to maintain that reputation over time. Specialized sellers are the safest option for high-value purchases.
  • eBay with caution: eBay's buyer protection helps, but the volume of reproductions on the platform is high. Focus on sellers with high feedback scores, detailed photos showing the actual item (not stock images), and a history of selling retro games. Use the sold listings filter to verify that a price is consistent with the authentic market, not the reproduction market.
  • Local game stores: A physical store where you can inspect the cartridge in person and ask questions about provenance is invaluable for expensive purchases. Many retro game stores will open a cartridge for you so you can verify the board.
  • Collector communities: Subreddits like r/gameverifying provide free authentication help from experienced collectors. Post photos of the label, back shell, and circuit board, and the community will help you determine authenticity before you finalize a purchase.
Investment in tools: A 3.8mm gamebit screwdriver, a 4.5mm gamebit screwdriver, and a small magnifying loupe represent a total investment of about $15 to $20. These three tools will let you authenticate virtually any cartridge from the NES through the GBA era. Consider them as fundamental to your collecting toolkit as the consoles themselves.

Reproductions Are Not Always Bad

It is worth noting that reproductions have a legitimate place in the hobby when sold honestly at reproduction prices. If a game costs $200 or more for an authentic copy and you simply want to play it on original hardware, a $10 reproduction cartridge serves that purpose. The problem is exclusively when reproductions are sold deceptively as authentic games at authentic prices. Knowing how to tell the difference protects you from paying authentic prices for reproduction products, and that knowledge is what separates an informed collector from an easy target.
